# Pass 0 — Historical frame

The article is written after guerrilla warfare has already expanded in North China and before `论持久战`.

It begins from a classification puzzle:

```text
guerrilla war is auxiliary at whole-war level
but
under China's specific conditions it has strategic-scale consequences
```

This makes the text especially useful for testing **level-dependent importance**, **special-case rule rewriting**, **state transitions**, and **centralization/decentralization**.

# Pass 1 — Source / argument reconstruction

## 1.1 A globally secondary component can still require strategic treatment

The text explicitly keeps regular warfare as the principal form and guerrilla warfare as auxiliary, yet argues that guerrilla warfare has escaped a merely tactical role because of China's specific configuration:

- large territory;
- relative national weakness;
- small-but-strong invading state;
- insufficient enemy manpower for broad occupation;
- prolonged war;
- large occupied spaces with gaps;
- organized political/military forces and mass population.

Thus:

```text
role at system level = auxiliary
but
scale/duration/autonomy of subproblem = strategic
```

Importance cannot be inferred from a single hierarchy label.

## 1.2 General strategy cannot be copied unchanged into a special subsystem

The article asks directly why general anti-Japanese strategy cannot simply be applied to guerrilla war and answers that common principles exist but special characteristics require special rules.

This is one of the clearest source-level anti-template statements in the corpus.

## 1.3 A high-level objective generates multiple operating principles

The core military objective is expressed as preserving/developing one's own force while reducing the enemy, in service of the political objective.

Six strategic problem clusters are then derived:

1. offensive action within strategic defense / quick action within protracted war / exterior action within strategic interior position;
2. coordination with regular war;
3. base-area creation;
4. strategic defense/offense;
5. development toward maneuver warfare;
6. command relations.

Again, a principal objective does not reduce implementation to one lever.

## 1.4 Rules reverse across levels without contradiction

The text systematically distinguishes:

```text
whole-war strategic defense
vs
campaign/tactical offense

whole-war protracted duration
vs
battle-level quick decision

whole-war interior lines
vs
local exterior encirclement
```

This strongly confirms Volume-I P2 that opposite rules can both be correct when object/level differs.

## 1.5 Initiative is produced by model quality and action, not possessed as a fixed trait

The article defines initiative as freedom of action and says it comes from:

```text
correct estimate of objective conditions
+
correct military/political disposition
```

Both pessimistic misestimation and over-optimistic adventurism can produce passivity.

This is a direct epistemic-control mechanism.

## 1.6 Weakness can sometimes create a capability unavailable to a stronger organization

The text notes that smallness/weakness can permit mobility and hidden activity unavailable to large regular forces.

This is not `weakness is strength` universally. It is:

```text
constraint
-> changes feasible action set
-> can create local advantage under matching environment
```

## 1.7 Flexibility and planning are treated as complements

The text combines:

```text
主动
灵活
有计划
```

Flexibility concerns adaptation of force use to task, enemy, terrain and population conditions, while planning preserves purpose and coordination.

A modern rendering would be:

```text
plan = stable intent/constraints
flexibility = local policy adaptation
```

not `plan everything` or `improvise everything`.

## 1.8 Base-area state is explicitly modeled as reversible

The article distinguishes:

```text
base area
contested guerrilla zone
enemy-controlled area
```

and says transitions can occur in both directions depending on military pressure and leadership error.

This is a concrete finite-state model with regression.

## 1.9 Base creation requires several jointly relevant conditions

The article names three basic conditions:

1. armed force;
2. defeat/contain the enemy;
3. mobilize/organize the population;

plus geographic and economic conditions.

Important distinction:

- some conditions are naturally given/background constraints (e.g. national territory size);
- some must be actively built (force, organization, governance, policy).

This is a much richer condition model than `find one bottleneck`.

## 1.10 Development and consolidation are alternating priorities

The text explicitly rejects both:

```text
only expand
and
only consolidate
```

and says different periods should shift emphasis depending on state.

This is a dynamic exploration/exploitation-like balance, though we should not import modern terminology into source interpretation.

## 1.11 Strategic command should be centralized while concrete action is decentralized

This is perhaps the most important organizational architecture in the text:

```text
strategic/general matters
-> centralized coordination/reporting/guidance

campaign/battle concrete action
-> decentralized local authority
```

Why?

Because distant upper levels cannot know rapidly changing local conditions.

The article explicitly states that greater area, complexity and distance should increase local independent authority.

This is an information-locality theory of delegation.

## 1.12 Growth from guerrilla to maneuver/regular force is capability transformation, not simple size increase

The text requires:

```text
quantity expansion
+
quality improvement across
politics / organization / equipment / technology / tactics / discipline
```

It also warns that building a main force should not destroy local forces that feed/support it.

Thus scaling is ecosystem architecture, not mere aggregation.

# Pass 4 — Adversarial reading

1. The military context has adversarial/destructive objectives; modern organizational transfer must abstract control structure, not combat content.
2. The principle `preserve self / destroy enemy` cannot be generalized to non-zero-sum domains.
3. Claims about enemy weaknesses and eventual passivity are partly prospective and require prediction audit.
4. `Correct estimate -> initiative` underplays uncontrollable shocks and structural disadvantage.
5. Local autonomy can improve responsiveness but also fragment policy, create drift, or hide abuse; accountability mechanisms are underdeveloped.
6. Strategic centralization can preserve coordination but upper-level strategy itself may be wrong; the text gives weaker mechanisms for bottom-up strategic challenge than for local tactical autonomy.
7. Base-area condition lists may be incomplete and context-specific.
8. Later editorial notes that confirm flatland base viability are hindsight evidence and should not be folded into original confidence.
9. `From guerrilla to regular` may be directionally appropriate in this setting but not a universal maturity path for decentralized systems.

# First-pass takeaway

The deepest transferable architecture is not a military tactic. It is a control-design pattern:

```text
start from global objective
-> preserve subsystem specificity
-> model states and conditions
-> keep strategic/shared constraints coordinated
-> move concrete authority toward local information
-> switch expansion/consolidation priorities by state
-> scale without destroying local feeder capability
-> revise when the system regresses or the environment changes
```

This is one of the strongest candidates so far for future Agent/organizational integration, but must be tested outside military command before promotion.
